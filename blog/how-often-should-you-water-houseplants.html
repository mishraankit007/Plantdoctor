How Often Should You Really Water Houseplants?

Plant Doctor Team · Plant Care Guides

"Water once a week" is the most repeated — and most misleading — piece of houseplant advice out there. The truth is there's no universal schedule, because how fast soil dries out depends on far more than the calendar. Here's what actually determines when your plant needs water, and a method that works for any plant you own.

Why a fixed schedule fails

The same plant in the same pot can need water every 4 days in summer and every 3 weeks in winter. A terracotta pot dries out much faster than plastic. A plant in a bright south-facing window drinks faster than one in a dim corner. A root-bound plant in a tiny pot dries out faster than one with room to spread. None of that shows up on a "water every Sunday" reminder — which is exactly how overwatering, the single most common way houseplants die, happens.

The only test that actually works: check the soil, not the calendar

Stick a finger into the soil up to your second knuckle (about 2 inches). For most common houseplants:

  • Dry at 2 inches down → water thoroughly now
  • Still damp/cool to the touch → wait a few more days, check again

This single habit replaces the need for any fixed schedule, because it responds to your plant's actual conditions in real time — not a guess made when you bought it.

How different plant types change the answer

Succulents & cacti

Let the soil go completely dry, then water deeply. These store water in their leaves and stems — chronic dampness is the fastest way to kill them.

Tropical foliage (monstera, pothos, philodendron)

Let the top 1–2 inches dry, then water thoroughly until it drains out the bottom. Don't let them sit bone-dry for long stretches either — they prefer consistent, moderate moisture.

Ferns & calatheas

These like the soil to stay lightly, consistently moist — check more often (every 2–3 days) and water in smaller amounts rather than letting them fully dry out.

Orchids

Water only when the roots (visible through a clear pot) turn from green to silvery-white, usually once a week — orchid bark dries faster than it looks.

Signs you're overwatering

Yellowing leaves that feel soft or mushy, soil that stays wet for over a week, a sour or musty smell from the pot, or gnats hovering around the soil surface. If you see these, let the plant dry out further than usual before watering again, and check the drainage hole isn't blocked.

Signs you're underwatering

Crispy brown leaf edges, leaves drooping but perking back up quickly after watering, and soil that's pulled away from the pot's sides. This one's easier to recover from than overwatering — just increase frequency slightly and monitor.

The bottom line

Stop watering on a fixed day of the week. Check the soil, factor in your pot material and light, and adjust with the seasons. Once you build the habit of checking rather than scheduling, most common houseplant problems — including the majority of yellow-leaf and root-rot cases — become far less likely in the first place.
